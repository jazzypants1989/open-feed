# Encrypted-content (Option E) — prototype findings

> **Status update (v0.2.0): superseded in part.** The direction was adopted —
> `open-feed-encrypted-content.md` is the resulting extension. Three claims below were
> found wrong or overstated in review and are corrected in place, marked **[CORRECTED]**.
> Read the extension for what was actually specified; read this for how it got there.
>
> Status: **feasibility probe, not a spec commitment.** Generated by `tmp/enc-prototype.js`
> (`node tmp/enc-prototype.js` → ALL CLAIMS PASS). Purpose: test the five-pass claim that an
> encrypted item is "just a signed item whose content is a JWE envelope" and that the core needs
> only two additive hooks, *before* deciding whether to delete the restricted-feeds extension.

## What was proven

A "restricted" item was built as an **ordinary Open Feed signed item** with `content_text:""`
and an `_enc` field carrying a **JWE JSON Serialization** (`alg:ECDH-ES+A256KW`, `enc:A256GCM`,
X25519 per RFC 8037), encrypted to two recipients (Dad, Kid). Verified end-to-end:

| Claim | Result | How |
|-------|--------|-----|
| 1. Signs with construction #1 | **PASS** | reused the *exact* `sign()` from `regen.js` — no new signing path |
| 2. Verifies with the unchanged verifier | **PASS** | reused the *exact* `verify()` — signer/host never touches plaintext |
| 3. An ordinary manifest commits the ciphertext | **PASS** | manifest `items[id]=_version` as usual; host serves bytes it can't read |
| 4. Only intended recipients decrypt | **PASS** | Dad + Kid recover plaintext; a stranger's key is locked out |

So the headline structural claim holds: **encryption is a client-side content-encoding, not a
new trust construction.** Signing, author binding, `_feed_url`, tombstones, and the manifest all
operate on the encrypted bytes unmodified. The feed can stay world-readable, CORS-`*`, statically
hosted — the exact "one concept that scales" property (single-hub, cross-hub, and dumb-CDN are all
*identical*: the host always just serves signed bytes).

## The honest corrections to the five-pass summary

**1. [CORRECTED] The "two additive hooks" count was wrong in both directions.**
Adversarial review counted **seven** touches, not two — adding a tombstone rule, an
enc-key lifecycle sub-section, a recipient-count cap, security-considerations text, and
a verifier-side binding rule. The resolution in v0.2.0 was different again: by scoping
core §4.1's `crv`/`use` constraints to *signing* keys, the core needs **zero** touches
that mention encryption. Every item on the seven-touch list lives in the extension. The
original text follows for the record.

**1a. The original claim, as written:**
The passes said an X25519 enc key "is already additive under §4.1's ignore-unrecognized-`crv`
clause." That clause (spec line 167) only guarantees *backward-compatibility* — an old verifier
won't choke on the key. It does **not** define the key. The §4.1 table (lines 159–164) currently
says `crv` MUST be `Ed25519` and `use` ∈ `{sig, recovery}`. To *specify* an enc key you must
**edit that table** to admit `crv:X25519` + `use:"enc"`. So the real core touch is:
   - **(a) an edit to §4.1** defining the `use:"enc"` X25519 key (not a zero-touch add), and
   - **(b) one sentence in §7** that an item's content MAY be a JWE envelope in `_enc` with
     `content_text:""`.
Still small — two localized edits — but "the core is untouched" is not accurate. Call it two
*small additive edits*.

**2. Metadata leak surface is real and worth stating in the README, not buried.** What a passive
host or non-recipient still sees in cleartext, by design: `id`, `date_published`, `_feed_url`,
`authors`, `_version`. Only `content` is opaque. Untagged recipients (no `kid` per recipient) also
hide *who* the audience is — a strict improvement over every current restricted-feeds mode, all of
which disclose the audience or the feed's gated existence. But encryption hides *content*, not
*that you posted, when, and in reply to what*. It does **not** serve the "hide the feed's
existence" persona.

**3. Envelope cost is modest.** The 2-recipient encrypted item canonicalized to ~1042 bytes
(vs ~250 for the plaintext equivalent). Overhead scales ~linearly in recipient count (one wrapped
CEK + one ephemeral pubkey per recipient, a few hundred bytes each). At family N≈10, negligible
against post+media. Confirmed "trivial."

## What was NOT proven — and is the real open question

The prototype covers **single-author broadcast** (Mom → her family), which is the journaling
product's spine and the clean case. It does **not** cover **replies to an encrypted item**, and
that is the genuinely-hard part the passes disagreed on (pass 4: "research frontier"; pass 5:
"shippable v2"):

> A reply to an encrypted item must be encrypted **to the same audience**, or it leaks content by
> inference. But the replier is a *reader*, not the author — and with untagged recipients we
> **deliberately hid the audience** (finding 2). So the replier doesn't know whom to wrap to.

This is the group-membership problem in miniature. The options, honestly:
- **v1 broadcast-first:** encrypted posts + audience-of-one DMs (true E2E) only; replies to an
  encrypted item either happen in cleartext referencing the id (leaking only that a reply exists)
  or via a trusted hub. Small, shippable, honest.
- **v2 circles:** the owner (who *is* the roster authority for its own audience) distributes an
  **encrypted roster** to members; a reply wraps to the current roster version (itself a
  chained/pinned document, so rollback is caught by the same discipline as everything else).
  No forward secrecy, no retroactive revocation — but that is the *identical* concession
  restricted-feeds §2 already makes ("authorized readers can re-share"). Tractable, but real work,
  and it should be **prototyped the same way before it's promised.**

## The boundary to state loudly — [CORRECTED]

The rule is right: encryption is confidential against **every party except (1) the plaintext's
author and (2) a holder of a recipient's private enc key.** The author needs no secret to encrypt
(they encrypt to recipients' *public* keys), so the author's own key custody is irrelevant.

**The conclusion originally drawn from it was wrong.** This document claimed encryption protects
your content on "the CDN, the network, and **every other family's hub** — even for hub-managed
users." That does not follow, because for a hub-managed recipient **the recipient's hub is a holder
of the recipient's private enc key** (`DISTRIBUTION-MODEL.md`: hub-managed keys encrypted at rest;
hub-managed is the default). Something has to decrypt server-side to render a web page for a reader
on an iPad.

So between two hub-managed families the delivered guarantee is: hub A cannot read hub B's outbound
posts in transit or at rest on a CDN — which TLS and a non-public bucket largely provide already.
The "every other family's hub" line holds only when the *receiving* family is client-side-keyed.
The honest statement, now §1 of the extension: **encryption's value tracks exactly how many
recipients hold their own enc keys**, which is a product/UX variable, not a protocol one.

It remains "theater" against **your own authoring hub, for your own posts** (which it wrote, so it
has the plaintext regardless). For the AI product
specifically: if the AI companion is a wrapped recipient, its provider *can* read — encryption
makes that a visible wrap-list entry, not a policy promise.

## Circles / encrypted interaction — prototyped (`tmp/circles-prototype.js`)

The reply-to-encrypted-item problem was then prototyped the same way. Roster = a **chained,
signed, encrypted** document: the owner wraps the member list `[{identity, x25519}]` to each
member's published X25519 key, versioned with `seq`/`prev`. `node tmp/circles-prototype.js` →
ALL CLAIMS PASS:

| Claim | Result |
|-------|--------|
| Roster is an encrypted signed doc — members decrypt, non-members locked out | **PASS** (no chicken-and-egg: same trial-decrypt as a post; owner wraps to *published* keys) |
| A **reader** learns the audience from the roster and wraps a reply the whole audience reads | **PASS** — the exact thing broadcast could not do |
| Member added at v2 can't read a v1-wrapped reply (no history access) | **PASS** |
| Member removed at v3 can't read v3 content, but keeps v1 content already fetched | **PASS** (no retroactive revoke — identical to the §2 "readers can re-share" concession) |
| Roster **rollback** (host serves stale v2 to re-add a removed member) is rejected | **PASS** — because the roster is a chain; same pin-and-walk as identity/manifest |

So the elegance claim substantially **holds through interaction**: circles reuse the pieces already
on the table (encrypt-to-published-keys + a chained/pinned document). This is closer to pass 5's
"shippable v2" than pass 4's "research frontier."

### But three residual limits — name them, don't pretend they're solved

1. **The roster-freshness race (genuinely new; neither pass named it).** A replier wraps to the
   roster version *they* currently hold. If the owner removed Dad at v3 but the replier is still on
   v2, the reply includes Dad. Rollback detection does **not** catch this — the replier honestly
   holds a stale-but-valid pin. So a just-removed member can receive in-flight replies during the
   propagation window. This is the eventual-consistency tax of any no-PFS group; bounded and
   acceptable at family scale, but it must be stated.
2. **Enc-key ↔ identity binding must be wired through published identity docs.** The prototype used
   freestanding X25519 keys. In the real design, each member's enc key MUST be the `use:"enc"` key
   in *their own published identity document*, so a replier can cross-check a roster entry against
   the member's identity doc — otherwise the owner could substitute a key it controls. This is a
   required integration point, not optional.
3. **No forward secrecy.** Compromise of a member's long-term X25519 key decrypts all past
   ciphertext wrapped to it. Same as any no-PFS messaging; a real limitation to disclose.

## The defect the probes missed — [ADDED]

Neither prototype tested it, and it is not a nitpick: **the JWE envelope was not bound to the item
carrying it.** Nothing inside the sealed bytes named the carrier, so anyone could lift `_enc` out of
a signed item, drop it into their own freshly-signed item with a fresh `id` and their own
authorship, and have an audience member decrypt and render the original author's private words
misattributed. Every core check passes. **The attacker need not be in the audience** — they relay
content they cannot read, which is strictly broader than cleartext copying.

Fixed: the sealed plaintext now self-attributes `id`/`authors`/`_feed_url` and the decrypting client
rejects any mismatch (extension §4.1). `tmp/enc-prototype.js` demonstrates the attack and the
rejection as CLAIM 5.

## Recommendation coming out of both probes

Both broadcast and circles prototype cleanly, with only *nameable, bounded* residual limits (the
freshness race, the enc-key binding, no PFS) — no dealbreaker surfaced. The "one concept that
scales" claim holds from single-author broadcast through multi-writer encrypted threads. That makes
the **E-forward** direction defensible, and **E-only** (encryption replaces restricted-feeds, with
existence/bandwidth as an operational note) reachable rather than reckless — *provided* the three
residual limits are written honestly into the extension and circles ship as an explicit v2, not a
v1 promise. The one thing that would still argue for keeping a thin audience-control mechanism
(E-complement) is a real need for **feed-existence privacy** or **host-trusted unencrypted**
cross-hub audiences — personas nobody has named across five passes plus two prototypes.
