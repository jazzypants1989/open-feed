# A. Attacking the other direction

## The attack: "enough peers a reader already trusts" is the divorce scenario, weaponized

Their key-change rule: an attestation "key B succeeds key A" is valid when signed by A, **or by enough peers a reader already trusts**. Their stated reasoning: at family scale identity continuity is social, and "a hostile operator cannot fool the subject's own mother."

The reasoning contains its own refutation. The brief's adversary is *inside the family* — which means, by construction, the hostile operator is a peer the mother already trusts. Bob isn't an outsider trying to fool grandma; he's her son-in-law of twenty years, his key is pinned in her reader, his feed is one she polls daily. He is *exactly* what "a peer a reader already trusts" denotes.

Concrete sequence, end to end:

1. Alice and Bob share the family hub. Every relative's reader follows both; Bob's signing key is pinned and trusted in all of them.
2. The divorce begins. Alice, per the scenario premise, hasn't moved — her identity lives on Bob's hub, and her PUT token still works, so Bob must act before she does.
3. Bob generates a keypair A′. He drafts the succession attestation: `{"succeeds": A, "new_key": A′, "seq": n+1}`, signed with his own key. Their threshold is open (their question 3), but any plausible family threshold is ≤ 3, and Bob has a colluding signer readily at hand: their nineteen-year-old son, who lives with Dad and is angry. Two peer signatures.
4. Bob publishes the attestation on his hub, where every relative's reader polls Alice.
5. Grandma's reader evaluates: two signers, both keys pinned and trusted, threshold met → **accept**. Alice's identity now resolves to A′ *inside grandma's reader*, with fully valid cryptography.
6. Bob publishes posts signed by A′. Every signature verifies. "Alice" announces she wants nothing to do with the family, recants things she said, whatever Bob wants. Floor assurance 1 — *nothing the host serves as yours verifies unless you signed it* — is violated not by breaking the crypto but by the crypto working exactly as specified.
7. Alice's real key still works, and her own counter-rotation would also validate — but she has no other location (the scenario premise: everything lives on his hub), her PUT token is now revoked, and the readers who matter poll Bob's hub exclusively. Her counter-attestation is unreachable. For the family, it does not exist.

The team's defense inverts: the mother isn't fooled *despite* the mechanism, she is fooled *by* it. The mechanism was designed for "your peers vouch that you lost your key"; its validity predicate as written is "anyone the **observer** likes." Delegation anchored in the observer is not delegation.

There's a structural corollary that's almost as bad: **identity becomes viewer-relative.** The sister who never followed Bob's feed doesn't trust him, rejects the attestation, and keeps the old key. Now the family holds two incompatible verdicts about who Alice is, each locally valid. My design, at its worst, produces a *visible* fork ("identity contested, two live chains"). Theirs produces a *silent, inconsistent* one — resolved-looking, resolved differently per reader. That's worse, because nothing prompts anyone to make the phone call.

The fix is one sentence — the attesting peers must have been **designated by the key being succeeded**, recorded in the identity, not in the reader — and it is the sentence their design is missing.

## Second failure: the failover that can never fire

Their relocation rule: "readers remember every location a profile has ever named and check the others when the primary goes stale." Their completeness rule: "deliberately no freshness deadline."

These two cannot coexist. *Stale* is the trigger for failover, and with no freshness semantics, a hostile hub serving a frozen-but-valid head is byte-for-byte indistinguishable from an author who stopped posting. The trigger condition is undefinable, so the failover never fires. Scenario 1 requires that Bob's frozen copy "reads as stale rather than as her" — for every relative who never learns of the move, Alice simply went quiet in March, and Bob gets to narrate the silence ("she's gone dark, poor thing"). The relocation mechanism — the part of their design that's genuinely good — is dead code without a staleness definition. (I'll concede in Part B that my design *claims* to solve this and doesn't.)

## Third: "BYOC is the security property" abdicates scenario 2

Bring-your-own-client is the right *author-side* property: a hub that never ships the only app never touches keys. But their specification "mandates none" of the backup UX, and the grandma scenario is a must-survive, not an app-store nice-to-have. Grandma cannot evaluate clients. Whoever chooses her app — the daughter, or in a worse family, the son-in-law "helping her set it up" — is her undeclared root of trust, and the spec is silent about it. The publish interface makes a client *market* possible; it does nothing for the chooser. My design made recovery-in-genesis a normative onboarding flow for exactly this reason; theirs explicitly disclaims it. That's a floor-assurance gap, not a UX preference.

## What they got right

A critique that finds nothing good isn't calibrated. Four of their choices are better than mine:

- **The publish interface.** I wrote "one authenticated PUT for the author" in passing and moved on. Their framing — clients and hubs form a market rather than a pairing — is the correct *reason* to specify it, and it's load-bearing for both the big lazy hub (a commercial hub can't pair you into its app) and the weekend (the third implementer needs a defined write surface). Their open question 1 is a better question than my answer.
- **Mandatory location memory with sequence precedence.** "Readers remember every location ever named; higher sequence wins" is *client law*, where my mirrors were *author prudence*. Mandatory beats advisory for exactly the users who need it most. (It needs the staleness trigger fixed, but the mechanism is right.)
- **One attestation shape.** My chain has typed events (`rotate`, `move`, profile change); theirs reduces key change, relocation, and profile edit to one shape with one verification rule. That's strictly more minimal at equal function, and minimalism is priority 4.
- **Extension discipline with stated constraints.** Their pull-only core, with push/DM/interop as named extensions each carrying restrictions ("rate-limit by IP before fetching, fetch only from known locations, never republish"), is more honest than my treatment, where DMs were nominally covered by "one construction, three uses" — a claim I'll retract in Part B. And their refusal of the "withheld/lag/stale verdict lattice" is correct; I half-built one rhetorically in my scenario 1 walkthrough.

Also right, and shared: identity-is-a-key, signatures over bytes as served, no canonicalization, generated views as the never-signed interop surface, and the candor that the stranger's resolver is an extension slot rather than pretending to solve it.

# B. Attacking my design

Seeing theirs exposed five real failures in mine, in rough order of embarrassment.

**1. My staleness claim was rhetoric, not mechanism.** I wrote that Bob's frozen copy "renders as stale, no updates since March, not as Alice going quiet now." False. A frozen signed index is byte-identical to a silent author. Nothing I specified distinguishes them. I had the same hole I just attacked them for, plus the dishonesty of claiming otherwise. Their mandatory all-location checking is the only real mitigation either of us has, and I made mine optional.

**2. My recovery model assumed the divorce away.** I wrote "her recovery contact is her sister, not him" — a smuggled assumption. During the marriage, Bob is the *most likely* designated recoverer: the onboarding flow asks "who can help if you lose your phone?" and the honest answer, for years, is your spouse. My design hands designated-Bob a clean, valid, recovery-signed rotation in the divorce, and my only mitigation was a "quiet provenance marker." The other team at least recognized social recovery as *the* hard problem and put it at the center; I treated it as solved by a UX flow. This is the single place they were thinking harder than I was. The fix I'll propose in Part C — a veto window plus a no-entrenchment rule — is a patch on a design that should have been built around the fact that **the hostile operator probably holds legitimate recovery standing.**

**3. No deletion semantics.** A personal publishing protocol where the author cannot say "remove this from my feed" is a privacy bug waiting for its first victim, and the divorce is full of them. I never wrote a word about tombstones, version history, or what "the publisher keeps" means. Their "publisher forgets; readers remember" stance is a real position; silence is not.

**4. My DM story is incoherent on delivery.** I said one encryption construction covers "circles, replies-in-circles, and DMs alike." Confidentiality, yes. Delivery, no: a DM in the *sender's* feed means the recipient must poll the sender's feed to discover it — poll everyone who might ever message you, or miss messages — and it means everyone polling the sender sees a DM-shaped ciphertext appear the night after the argument. I claimed coverage I didn't have. Their framing (DMs are an extension with constraints) is more honest and points toward the right shape.

**5. No head/index split.** My `feed.json` conflates two jobs: the completeness witness ("what exists, at what sequence") and the content index (the list with hashes). The consequences: every post rewrites and re-signs the whole file; cheap polling is impossible; pagination is bolted on. Their open question 4 identifies the seam even though they haven't closed it. The answer — a third tiny signed file — is in Part C.

Smaller items: my multi-device answer was "QR-to-QR, someone must specify it" (I didn't); their pre-signed scheduled-release idea is a genuinely nice device-trust insight I lacked entirely; and my dependency concession (standard-library-only is true in Go and false in Python) was left dangling where their question 2 pushes on the same seam from the security-review side. Neither of us resolved it.

**Where theirs is plainly better:** publish interface, mandatory location memory, attestation unification, extension discipline, deletion stance, the head/index question. **Where mine is plainly better:** delegation anchored in the identity rather than the observer (the Part A fix), the veto/contestation framing for forks, and normative onboarding flow for grandma rather than abdication to app quality.

# C. Reconcile

The two designs converged independently on the skeleton, which is weak evidence it's right: **identity is a key plus a signed event chain; artifacts are files signed as the bytes served, no canonicalization; the device is the only signer; the core is pull-only; one encryption mechanism serves all three tiers; generated feeds are the unsigned interop surface; the hub is static serving plus a publish interface.** The reconciliation takes their architecture and repairs its trust rules with mine, then adds what neither of us had.

## The merged identity and key-change model

One event shape, their unification applied to my chain — every chain event is the same document:

```json
{"v":1,"seq":14,"prev":"sha256 of event 13","key":"<current ed25519 pubkey>",
 "at":"2026-05-04T18:03:11Z","locations":["https://a.example/alice/","https://mirror.example/a/"],
 "name":"Alice","recovery":"sha256(recovery set commitment)"}
```

signed by the current key. Rotation, move, and rename are all just "the current key signs new state." Locations carry sequence precedence; **readers MUST retain every location ever seen and MUST prefer the highest-sequence valid event across all of them** — their rule, made mandatory, with the staleness trigger defined below.

**Recovery, rebuilt around the actual adversary.** Three rules, each aimed at a failure named above:

1. **Delegation is anchored in the identity.** A peer-signed succession counts only if the signers are members of a recovery set the *superseded key* designated. The set is committed in the chain as a hash — `sha256(pubkey₁ ‖ … ‖ pubkeyₙ)` — and revealed only inside a recovery event. Pre-recovery, the hub and strangers learn nothing about who the recoverers are (their question 3's instinct — "never publish the trust set" — is right, and the commitment is how you get it); at recovery time, the reveal is checked against the commitment. Grandma designates her daughter at onboarding and the designation is invisible until used. Bob-the-colluding-peer has no standing he wasn't given.
2. **The veto window.** A recovery-signed succession is *provisional* for a fixed window (default: 14 days). During the window, the superseded key may sign a `contest` event; any reader who sees one displays "identity contested" prominently and trusts neither branch pending social resolution. This is my patch for my own failure B2: if Bob *was* the designated recoverer and rotates Alice's key to his own, Alice — still holding her key, since recovery exists for *loss*, and she hasn't lost hers — vetoes within the window, and every polling reader sees the contest. It converts my "resolution is social" hand-wave into a mechanism with a deadline.
3. **No entrenchment.** A recovery-signed event may change `key` and nothing else. Not locations, not the recovery commitment, not the name. A thief with stolen recovery standing gets one provisional key change, loudly marked, and cannot rewrite where the identity lives or who can resurrect it next. Alice's escape routes survive the attack.

This keeps their one-attestation-shape simplicity and my anchoring, and it costs one extra state ("provisional") in the reader — I think that's the cheapest honest answer to the contested departure anywhere in this design space. The residual case — Alice has genuinely lost her key *and* Bob was her designated recoverer — is not cryptographically solvable, and the design's answer is the fingerprint, below.

**Staleness, finally defined.** Three parts, weakest-link honest: (a) sequence precedence across all remembered locations is the hard rule; (b) the author's app **pre-signs sparse heartbeat events** — their scheduled-post insight, repurposed: one tiny "still here" event per week for the next six months, released on schedule by the hub. A hostile hub can withhold them, but any reader who checks a second location — mandatory behavior — sees the heartbeat there; (c) clients apply a heuristic staleness flag when the primary's timestamp is older than k× the author's longest observed posting gap, and *check the other locations* — that's the trigger their failover was missing. It's a heuristic, and I flag it as one: it detects frozen hubs for readers who remember a second location and for no one else. Strangers remain inside the conceded non-goal.

## The publish interface (their question 1, answered)

Conventional paths, session capability, no per-request signatures:

```
GET  {base}/.pub/nonce                → {"nonce":"<32 random bytes>"}
POST {base}/.pub/session  {"id":"<pubkey>","sig":"ed25519(nonce ‖ 'session')"}
                                      → {"token":"<opaque>"}
PUT  {base}/.pub/files/head.json      (Bearer token)
PUT  {base}/.pub/files/items/<sha256> (Bearer token)
PUT  {base}/.pub/files/blobs/<sha256> (Bearer token)
```

The hub binds the path prefix to the identity that opened the session; the token is a revocable capability that cannot forge content; the hub stores no key, ever. There is no `DELETE` — deletion is a new head, which keeps hubs as dumb as possible. Server side is ~50 lines. This is their contribution, specified; I adopt it wholesale.

## The head (their question 4, answered)

**The head is a third tiny signed file; the generated feed view is never the head — that should be a normative MUST.** Two reasons: a generated view can be regenerated by a hostile hub, and the head must be something only the author can produce; and a cheap poll fetches bytes, not a feed. Shape:

```json
{"v":1,"seq":214,"prev":"…","key":"…","locations":[…],
 "index":"sha256(index.json)","removed":["<up to 50 recent tombstone hashes>"],"at":"…"}
```

signed by the current key. `index` points at signed index pages (pagination falls out cleanly). `removed` is the deletion mechanism: their tombstone stance, bounded — big enough for any reader on cadence to see what vanished, small enough to be no one's permanent ledger. The publisher keeps no history; readers keep what they fetched.

For the unsigned surface: the generated **JSON Feed embeds each item's signature envelope in an extension field** (`"_sig"`), so any JSON-Feed-aware consumer can verify without extra round trips — JSON Feed is extensible JSON, so this costs nothing. RSS/Atom get the item's sha256 as the `guid` — a pin, not a proof. The generated views stay unverifiable to dumb readers; that wound is shared by both designs and neither of us closes it. This is the best partial dressing I know.

## DMs (their question 5, answered)

Kill the push ping. Its metadata cost and spam surface buy latency that families don't need. Replace the DM extension with a **dead-drop box**: the head names a box URL — which *need not be the content hub*, letting a user decorrelate the two. The sender blind-PUTs one instance of the standard construction (single recipient envelope) to `{box}/{client-chosen random token}`. The recipient polls their own box — everything remains pull from the recipient's perspective; the sender performs one blind storage write, which is the minimum any delivery system can be. Box policy: per-IP rate limit, per-object size cap, TTL garbage collection; spam filtering is client-side. "Never republish what was delivered" becomes a client rule: the DM moves from box to local archive and never appears in any feed. The hub learns a write happened, from some IP, of some size — the same leakage class as everything else, stated rather than hidden.

## What neither design considered

**1. The human verification layer.** Both designs resolve their hardest cases socially — "she calls her daughter," "the mother has an out-of-band channel" — and neither gave the humans a *protocol artifact*. Add one: a **verbal fingerprint**, the first 40 bits of the genesis hash rendered as eight digits or three words ("marble-violet-72"). Families compare it by phone the way they already compare nothing else cryptographic. It costs zero crypto, it is the cheapest possible defense against TOFU introductions and contested forks, and it converts both designs' shared hand-wave — "resolution is social" — into a step a person can actually perform. Neither of us had it. I think it's the single highest-value addition in this whole reply.

**2. The hub's *view* needs witnesses.** Both designs are strictly reader-vs-hub. A hub serving different valid states to different readers (split-view) is undetectable in both. The germ of an answer: optional **notary mirrors** — anyone can run a service that fetches a hub's view and republishes the head hashes it saw, signed. Not core, not required, no consensus — but the *framing*, that the hub's serving behavior itself is an object readers may want attested, is absent from both designs, and it's the residual hole under assurance 1.

**3. Time discipline, stated once.** Both designs use timestamps everywhere and never say what clocks are *for*. The rule I'd write: sequence and `prev` are the only ordering and validity inputs; signed `at` fields gate UX-level heuristics (staleness, veto windows) only; wall clock is never trusted for a security decision. One paragraph in the spec instead of an emergent muddle.

**4. The client is the unexamined root of trust.** Both designs say "the key never leaves the device" and both are silent on who chose the device's software. Neither can fix this cryptographically and I refuse to pretend otherwise; the honest moves are the ones this reconciliation makes — a real client market (publish interface), cheap escape (portability), and *visible* anomalous events (provisional rotations, contested forks, fingerprints) so a bad client's work doesn't stay quiet. Named, not solved.

**Problems that should not be solved at all**, since the invitation is explicit: the verdict lattice (their refusal was right; my scenario-1 rhetoric was half-building one, and I retract it); the push ping (killed above); the universal resolver (agreed non-goal); and audience-cardinality hiding — the envelope count leaks, both designs leak it, and chasing it costs real complexity for metadata the family adversary mostly has anyway.

# The five open questions

**1. Publish interface.** Answered above: conventional paths, challenge-response session, bearer capability, no DELETE. One thing I'd refuse to decide without more information: whether the session exchange needs a replay-nonce cache or can be stateless-timestamped — it depends on whether hubs are expected to run behind CDNs, which changes nonce semantics. Tell me the deployment picture.

**2. Encryption construction.** Split the question in two. *Don't* adopt an off-the-shelf **library** — it violates priority 1 and the interop story. *Do* adopt the off-the-shelf **construction shape**: X25519 → HKDF-SHA256 → AEAD is ECIES, the shape behind sealed boxes and `age`; spec it in thirty lines and have the composition reviewed — the reviewable surface is small *because* the construction is boring. The genuinely nonstandard part of my §6 — recipient hiding — is not crypto at all: it is the *absence of a recipient label* plus trial decryption, i.e., formatting. Keep it, but as the flagged, separable piece it is. I would refuse to finalize this answer without knowing the target implementation languages: if the implementer base is Python, where none of this is in the standard library, "audited off-the-shelf library" becomes the correct trade and priority 1 needs renegotiating, not silent violation. That's the single piece of missing information that changes my answer.

**3. Recovery threshold and trust-set publication.** Reframe, then answer. The concept "peers a reader trusts" should not exist in the specification at all — that's the Part A attack. What exists is a **designated recovery set, chosen by the key, committed as a hash, revealed only on use**. Threshold: default 1 (grandma's daughter); k-of-n is expressible in the commitment but I'd cut it from v1 — the commitment format allows it later without a migration. Publication: never, except inside a recovery event, where the reveal is forced and checked against the commitment. Their "never publish the trust set" instinct was right; the hash commitment is the mechanism that makes it true rather than aspirational.

**4. What is the head.** Firmly: a third tiny signed file, and the generated feed is *never* the head — normative MUST. A generated view is regenerable by the hostile hub; the head must be producible only by the author's key. The split also buys cheap polling, clean pagination, the tombstone list, and an object for the verbal fingerprint and notaries to refer to. This is the one question of the five where I think there is a single right answer.

**5. Does the push channel exist.** No to the push ping; yes to the dead-drop box, as the DM extension, with the box location named in the head and permitted to differ from the content hub. Everything the recipient does remains pull; the sender does one blind ciphertext write. Inbound fediverse replies stay a bridge extension, nothing more. The metadata the box leaks — a write occurred, from some IP, of some size — is the floor for any delivery mechanism that exists, and I'd rather state it than push it into a channel with more machinery and the same leak.
